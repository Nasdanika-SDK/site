Preface

This book is about Meta, the language-neutral type system of the Nasdanika SDK Runtime: a micro-model of typed domain models, with packages, classes, features, operations and the type references between them, in about twenty classifiers. A vocabulary is written down once in Meta, with its documentation as structure, and projected to whatever its consumers need: a glossary or a PDF for people, a dynamic Ecore package for tools, TypeScript for browsers, JSON Schema for the tools of agents. Developers write it in Meta’s text syntax, .nmeta files. The people who own the words can have an AI assistant write it for them today, with the build checking the structure and themselves the meaning; they will also write it in the spreadsheets, diagrams and Markdown they already use.

The first part of the book is the why: why one definition should not live in many formats, why documentation should be structure, what Meta leaves out on purpose, and why there is no server. The second part is the how, for Java developers: a vocabulary of a library, built in Java; the vocabulary of a bank, with its customers, products, accounts, transactions and statements, written in the text syntax and loaded through a resource set; and projections of both to a glossary and to Ecore. It ends with domain design with an AI assistant: who checks what, how a subject-matter expert works with one, the validator and the agent skill. The third says what comes next: the formats vocabularies will be authored and published in, with who each is for, flattening, and vocabularies that vary by where and when they apply.

Who this book is for

Architects and modelers who keep a domain’s vocabulary, today in spreadsheets, wiki tables and schemas that disagree, and want it in one place with the types and the documentation generated from it.

Developers who write vocabularies in the text syntax, and projections from a vocabulary to code, schemas, documentation, or the context of an agent.

Business owners of a vocabulary and other subject-matter experts, who will read Part I, the first half of Chapter 11, which is how they can take part today with no code, and Chapter 12: what they will author in, and what they will get back.

Part I: The Why

Why a vocabulary should be written down once, why its documentation should be structure, what Meta leaves out, and why there is no server. No code is needed to read this part.

Chapter 1. One definition, many spellings

A class with named, typed, possibly multi-valued features is one of the most stable ideas in computing, and every ecosystem spells it its own way: Ecore, Java, XML Schema, JSON Schema, a database schema, TypeScript, a table on a wiki page, a spreadsheet. The differences are real and small, and most of them are about the host language, not about the domain.

The cost is not the spelling. It is that one definition, of a customer, an order or a service, lives in many formats at once and nothing relates them: a DDL script, a JSON Schema, a partner’s XSD, a Java class, a wiki table and a few spreadsheets. Each is a projection of a domain model that was never written down. Meta is the place to write it down once, and project.

1.1. A published language

In the terms of Domain-Driven Design, Meta is a Published Language: a documented, shared vocabulary that bounded contexts translate into and out of. It adds the part that was out of reach when the term was coined: the vocabulary is machine-checkable, because the types and the documentation are generated from it.

It is deliberately not a superset of any of its sources. Whatever does not fit is dropped by design or carried in an annotation, and the loaders decide which (Chapter 3).

1.2. Publishing is the deliverable

A model that cannot be read by the people who own the vocabulary is a private notation. A vocabulary published as a site, with a page per element, its documentation, its neighbors and a diagram of its neighborhood, is a glossary that cannot drift from the types, because both come from one source. That is what a business stakeholder opens, and nobody has to hear the word "metamodel".

1.3. Agents need structure

An assistant reasoning over an organization’s systems has to know what a customer is, what an account is, and how they relate. Today that context is assembled by hand, per project, from whatever prose can be found. An agent given a declared, versioned, owned vocabulary answers from it and cites it; an agent given a DDL script and a wiki export infers, and inference is not a chain of custody.

1.4. The incumbent is a spreadsheet

The competitor is not another modeling tool. It is a spreadsheet and a wiki table, which win because they are free, everyone can use them, and they never ask anyone to adopt anything. Meta treats them as inputs, not as enemies: the vocabulary is captured where it already lives, and projected from there PLANNED. Developers get a text syntax of their own, and everybody gets the outputs they read, down to a PDF for a partner (Chapter 12). Until those inputs are here, the people who own the words can have an assistant write the text syntax for them, and check what it says about their business (Chapter 11).

Chapter 2. Documentation as structure

Most formats carry documentation as one string, and some do better. The claim worth making is narrower than "nobody supports documentation":

Source Documentation it carries

Ecore

One string, in a GenModel annotation detail

Java

Nothing reflective: Javadoc lives in source comments, not in the type model

JDBC

REMARKS, one string per table or column, often empty

JSON Schema

A title and a description

Protocol Buffers

Source comments, and only when descriptors are built to keep them

XML Schema

xs:annotation/xs:documentation: repeatable, tagged with a language, markup permitted

OpenAPI

description in CommonMark, and externalDocs

OWL and RDFS

rdfs:comment, skos:definition, extensible and tagged with a language

Meta, through the core model

documentation, docFormat, docRef, docContents, docSections

XML Schema, OpenAPI and OWL do respectably. None of them treats documentation as structure. A string cannot be divided into sections, cannot point to a document that lives elsewhere, and cannot declare its own format.

Every Meta element is an element of the runtime’s core model, so its documentation can do all three:

  • documentation is text, in the format docFormat names, such as Markdown;

  • docRef points to a document elsewhere;

  • docContents and docSections structure it, so a glossary entry can have a definition, examples and notes for implementers as separate sections.

2.1. Where each element came from

Core model elements also have markers: where an element came from, a file and a position, or a Git repository, branch and commit. For a vocabulary, markers are the provenance story: this class from that sheet of that spreadsheet, at that commit; this feature from that element of that schema. When two sources disagree about what a term means, the model shows both, and where each came from.

Elements also have uris, alternative identifiers that let vocabularies maintained apart be assembled into one, and an icon.

Chapter 3. What Meta leaves out

The rule is that what a class-based type system cannot express is not a class in Meta. That keeps Meta at about twenty classifiers, against Ecore’s fifty-three, of which a loader at run time uses a few:

  • No volatile, transient, unsettable or resolveProxies: implementation concerns of EMF with no meaning in a browser.

  • No feature maps, no factories, no algebra of generic types. Unions, intersections and wildcards are carried, so that a Java or TypeScript signature can be written down without loss, but nothing computes subtyping or substitution with them.

  • No subpackages, which neither TypeScript modules nor npm packages need. A loader flattens them and records the original path.

  • No interface flag. Whether a class becomes an interface, a type alias or a class is the projection’s decision, not a property of the model.

3.1. Annotations, the bargain Ecore makes

Everything else arrives through an Annotation, an OpaqueTypeReference or a model that extends Meta. An annotation is keyed by a source URI that names its owner, the bargain Ecore’s EAnnotation makes, so two loaders never collide. Loaders record what they cannot map instead of dropping it, so a round trip to the source stays possible even though Meta is a projection of it.

3.2. Specializations per target

A projection sometimes needs to say something the neutral model should not carry. A Java projection adds a package name and a superclass; a persistence projection adds a table name; a TypeScript projection adds nothing at all. Those live in models that extend Meta, such as a JavaClass that extends Class, when they are structural enough to deserve types, and in annotations when they are not. The neutral model stays neutral either way, which is what lets one vocabulary serve many projections.

3.3. Why the JVM is not built on Meta

The runtime’s JVM model is the test of the rule. Bridge methods, synthetic accessors, access flags, and overloads that differ only in erased parameter types are realities of the JVM that Meta will not grow to hold. So the JVM model does not extend Meta; it projects to it PLANNED. A published language is a target of projections, not a base class.

Chapter 4. Federated by design

There is no server and no central repository. A vocabulary is files in Git, or an artifact published to Maven Central or npm, and packages reference each other by namespace URI. A team owns its vocabulary and refers to another team’s without coordinating a release, and gets what a catalog cannot give:

  • Branching. A proposed change to a term, reviewed as a pull request, with a diff that shows which features moved.

  • History. The same history the code has, with the same tools.

  • Ownership at the edges. Nobody administers the whole graph.

  • Immutable snapshots. What a term meant last year is an install, not an investigation.

4.1. Directly on the core model

Meta sits directly on the runtime’s core model, and on nothing else. What it needs from below is identity, documentation as structure and markers. That is deliberate: Meta is the foundation of every package generated from it, and a browser application should not drag a governance model in to render a list of customers, nor an agent pay for vocabulary it does not use in its context window on every call. Richer variants extend Meta, not the other way around.

The listings of this book register what they use by hand, with EMF alone.

5.2. Names that clash

Meta’s classes have the names of the concepts they model: Package, Class, Enum, Object. Package, Class, Enum and Object are also in java.lang, which every Java file imports. An explicit import of Meta’s class wins over java.lang in that file, as in the listings of this book, and the JDK’s class is then written with its package.

5.3. What the build does

The Xcore definition is in src/main/resources/org/nasdanika/sdk/runtime/models/meta/meta.xcore. Maven generates the Java sources into src-gen with the Xtext Maven plugin. The generated API is in three packages: the interfaces in org.nasdanika.sdk.runtime.models.meta, their implementations in impl, and the switch and adapter factory in util.

The grammar of the text syntax is in src/main/grammar/Meta.g4. The ANTLR Maven plugin generates its lexer and parser into src-gen as well, in the package parser, which is not exported: nothing outside the module sees a parse tree. The reader, the writer, the validator, the glossary, the resource and its factory are hand-written, in resource, and so are the package provider and the resource set contributor, in services.

Chapter 6. The model

Area Classifiers

The model is metacircular: a Package containing classes describes Package and Class, so a runtime can bootstrap from Meta alone and needs no foreign metamodel.

6.1. Base

Meta’s ModelElement extends the core model’s NamedElement, so every element has an id, a name, documentation as structure, markers, uris and an icon (Chapter 2). It contains annotations.

An Annotation has a source, a URI that names its owner, such as urn:org.nasdanika or the schema an element was loaded from, and details, a map of strings.

6.2. Structure

A Package is a namespace of classifiers, and the unit of publication: one package becomes one Maven artifact or one npm package. It has a namespace URI, its identity and what references resolve against, a short prefix, a version, and classifiers.

A Classifier is abstract and contains typeParameters, so that a data type may be parameterized too.

A Class is a type with features: what Ecore calls an EClass, what Java calls a class or an interface, and what JSON Schema calls an object schema. It has abstract, superTypes as type references, because a supertype may be parameterized, features and operations.

A DataType is a scalar type with a name of its own: Instant, Duration, EmailAddress. Its representation is how it crosses the wire and what a projection emits, an Instant is a string; its sourceType, such as java.time.Instant, is provenance only.

An Enum contains literals. An EnumLiteral has a serialized value, the name when unset, and an ordinal.

6.3. Members

A TypedElement has a type, a type reference, and a multiplicity: lowerBound, 0 for optional, and upperBound, 1 for single-valued and -1 for unbounded, with ordered and unique.

A Feature is state. derived means computed, not stored: a projection emits it as read-only or omits it. readonly is the positive form of Ecore’s changeable, and defaultValue is verbatim, in the serialized form of the type.

An Attribute has identifying: it identifies its instance, and a reference may cite it. That is how a Domain-Driven Design entity is told from a value object.

A Reference has containment, which separates the edges of a tree from the edges of a graph, an opposite for the other end of a bidirectional reference, and keys: the attributes that identify a target within the containing list.

An Operation is a signature of behavior, carried for fidelity when loading from Java or Ecore and rendered as documentation. It has typeParameters, parameters and exceptions; a Parameter may be variadic and has a defaultValue.

6.4. Types

A type reference stands in a type position. The set is small on purpose: enough to write a declaration, with an escape hatch.

Class What it references

ClassifierReference

A classifier: target within a model, or namespace and classifierName for one in a package the model does not contain, with typeArguments

TypeParameterReference

A type parameter, by target or parameterName

PrimitiveTypeReference

A PrimitiveKind

UnionTypeReference

One of several types: JSON Schema’s oneOf, an XSD choice, a TypeScript union

IntersectionTypeReference

All of several types; several bounds of a type parameter are one of these

WildcardTypeReference

OpaqueTypeReference

Verbatim text for what no class covers, such as conditional and mapped types, with the references it names, so that opaque text does not defeat import management

PrimitiveKind is a vocabulary of serialization, not of a language: STRING, NUMBER, BOOLEAN, BIGINT, DATE, TIME, DATE_TIME, DURATION, BINARY, ANY, NULL and VOID. NUMBER covers every numeric width, because JSON has one number and TypeScript has one number.

A projection without wildcards emits the bound that is set: the upper bound for ? extends T, the lower bound for ? super T, and the widest type it has for a bare ?, unknown in TypeScript.

Chapter 7. A vocabulary

The example of this book is the vocabulary of a library: people, books and the formats books are published in. It is built in Java, with three small builders.

7.1. Builders

Listing 2. Builders (Vocabulary.java)
static Class type(Package vocabulary, String name, String definition) {
    Class type = META.createClass();
    type.setName(name);
    type.setDocumentation(definition);                      // (1)
    vocabulary.getClassifiers().add(type);
    return type;
}

static Attribute attribute(
        Class owner,
        String name,
        PrimitiveKind kind,
        boolean identifying,
        String definition) {
    Attribute attribute = META.createAttribute();
    attribute.setName(name);
    attribute.setDocumentation(definition);
    attribute.setIdentifying(identifying);                  // (2)
    attribute.setLowerBound(identifying ? 1 : 0);
    PrimitiveTypeReference type = META.createPrimitiveTypeReference();
    type.setKind(kind);
    attribute.setType(type);                                // (3)
    owner.getFeatures().add(attribute);
    return attribute;
}

static Reference reference(Class owner, String name, Class target, String definition) {
    Reference reference = META.createReference();
    reference.setName(name);
    reference.setDocumentation(definition);
    reference.setUpperBound(-1);                            // (4)
    ClassifierReference type = META.createClassifierReference();
    type.setTarget(target);
    reference.setType(type);
    owner.getFeatures().add(reference);
    return reference;
}

  1. Documentation is the core model’s structure, not a string attribute of Meta.

  2. The attribute identifies its instance, as the identity of an entity in Domain-Driven Design.

  3. The type is a reference to a primitive kind, which is how a value crosses the wire.

  4. Many-valued.

7.2. The vocabulary

Listing 3. The library’s vocabulary (Vocabulary.java)
public static Package library() {
    Package vocabulary = META.createPackage();
    vocabulary.setName("library");
    vocabulary.setNamespace("https://example.org/vocabulary/library");
    vocabulary.setVersion("2026.10.0");
    vocabulary.setDocumentation("The words a library uses, and what they mean here.");

    Class person = type(vocabulary, "Person",
            "Someone who wrote, edited or translated a book.");
    attribute(person, "name", PrimitiveKind.STRING, true,
            "The name as printed on the title page.");

    Enum format = META.createEnum();
    format.setName("Format");
    format.setDocumentation("How a book is published.");
    for (String name: new String[] { "HARDCOVER", "PAPERBACK", "EBOOK" }) {
        EnumLiteral literal = META.createEnumLiteral();
        literal.setName(name);
        literal.setOrdinal(format.getLiterals().size());
        format.getLiterals().add(literal);
    }
    vocabulary.getClassifiers().add(format);

    Class book = type(vocabulary, "Book",
            "A published work, identified by its ISBN.");
    attribute(book, "isbn", PrimitiveKind.STRING, true,
            "International Standard Book Number, 13 digits.");
    attribute(book, "title", PrimitiveKind.STRING, false,
            "The title as printed, subtitle excluded.");
    attribute(book, "published", PrimitiveKind.DATE, false,
            "The date of the first edition.");
    reference(book, "authors", person,
            "The people credited as authors, in the order printed.");

    Annotation source = META.createAnnotation();                    // (1)
    source.setSource("https://example.org/catalog/schema");
    source.getDetails().put("table", "BOOKS");
    book.getAnnotations().add(source);
    return vocabulary;
}

  1. What a source expressed and Meta does not: here, the table a harvester found the books in.

7.3. Saving and loading

A vocabulary is a model, so it is saved, versioned, compared and reviewed like any other:

URI uri = URI.createFileURI(directory.resolve("library.xml").toString());
Resource resource = resourceSet().createResource(uri);
resource.getContents().add(Vocabulary.library());

  1. A file in a repository. A change to what a term means is a pull request whose diff shows which features moved.

The resource set of the listing registers the XMI factory and the Meta package by hand. In an application on the runtime, NasdanikaResourceSet.createAndConfigure() does both from the module path, registers the text syntax, and adds YAML and JSON when the runtime’s emf-json module is there.

Building a vocabulary in Java shows the model, and it is how a harvester or a projection builds one. It is not how a person should write one: the next chapter writes a bank’s vocabulary in the text syntax, and writes this library as text too.

Chapter 8. The text syntax

A vocabulary built in Java is a program that builds a vocabulary. A vocabulary written in Meta’s text syntax is the vocabulary itself: a file a developer reads top to bottom, diffs in a pull request, and edits in any editor.

The syntax writes metamodels the way Xcore writes Ecore models. Files have the extension .nmeta, one file is one package, and they are parsed by the ANTLR runtime alone, with no Xtext and no Eclipse. The grammar, src/main/grammar/Meta.g4, documents every construct and how it maps to the model.

The example of this chapter is the vocabulary of a bank: the products it sells, its customers, their accounts, the transactions posted to them and the statements that report them. It is the vocabulary an assistant over a bank’s systems needs before it can answer a question about any of them, and a business glossary the bank’s people can read.

8.1. A package and its imports

A file starts with its package:

Listing 5. The package of the bank (bank.nmeta)
/**
 * The words a bank uses with its customers, and what they mean here.
 *
 * Kept by the business and reviewed as a pull request, like the code it is generated into.
 */
package bank namespace "https://example.org/vocabulary/bank" prefix "bank" version "2026.10.0"

import "https://example.org/vocabulary/party" as party
import "https://runtime.sdk.nasdanika.org/models/occupancy" as occupancy

  • The package has a name, and its namespace URI is its identity: what other packages import, and what a reference to one of its classes resolves against. The prefix and the version are optional.

  • import names another package by its namespace, under an alias. party.Party is then the class Party of that package. A namespace is a name, not an address: nothing is fetched from it.

  • annotation declares an alias for an annotation source, so that @Nasdanika(icon = "bi-box") is an annotation with the source urn:org.nasdanika and a detail icon.

  • A documentation comment, /** …​ */, is the Markdown documentation of the element that follows it. Other comments are ignored.

A feature is a type followed by a name, as in Xcore:

  • With refers or contains it is a reference, and contains makes the target part of this object: a statement belongs to its account, a product does not.

  • Otherwise it is an attribute, of a primitive kind, such as string, date and dateTime, or of a data type or enumeration.

  • id marks an attribute that identifies its instance, as the identity of an entity in Domain-Driven Design.

  • opposite pairs two references, keys names the attributes that identify a contained object within its list, and = "fr" is a default value.

  • derived and readonly say that a feature is computed rather than stored, and op declares the signature of an operation. Meta carries no bodies.

8.6. Facets

An annotation is a bag of strings keyed by its source. A facet is typed: @occupancy.Presence { jurisdiction: "CA-QC" } says that the contract language of a customer applies in Quebec, with a class of the occupancy model as the type of what it says. Its value is an object literal, with strings, numbers, lists in [ ], nested objects and names of enumeration literals and elements as values.

A facet is checked against its type when the type is available: a property its class has no feature for is a warning. When the type is not available, as here, the facet is read without checking, and the warning says so.

Until the core model has facets, a facet is recorded as an annotation SKETCH: its source is the URI of its type, the namespace and the class name joined with #, its name is the class name, and its properties are details. An object value is a nested annotation named after its property, and a list one whose source is MetaReader.LIST, with its items keyed by position.

Listing 12. A facet, as recorded (TextSyntaxTests.java)
Class customer = (Class) classifier(bank, "Customer");

assertThat(presence.getSource())
    .isEqualTo("https://runtime.sdk.nasdanika.org/models/occupancy#Presence");

Roles, owners and occupancy are what facets are for: data about an element that another model defines and validates (Section 13.2).

8.7. Problems and provenance

The reader collects what is wrong in the resource’s getErrors() and getWarnings(), each with its file, line and column, and loading fails with the first error, as EMF’s XML resources do:

  • A syntax error leaves the resource empty.

  • An unknown type, an unknown alias, or an opposite or a key that does not exist is an error, and the resource keeps what could be read, so that a caller catching the exception still sees it.

  • A facet whose type is not available, or a property its type does not have, is a warning.

Every package, classifier, feature, operation, parameter and enum literal read from a file gets a core model marker with the file’s URI and the line:column where its declaration starts. When a generated page or a projected schema says something surprising, the marker says which line of which file it came from.

9.2. A dynamic Ecore package, for tools

Listing 21. An Ecore projection (Projections.java)
/**
 * A sketch of the projection to Ecore: classes, attributes and references, enough for EMF to
 * create, validate and serialize instances without generated code.
 */
public static EPackage ecore(Package vocabulary) {
    EcoreFactory ecore = EcoreFactory.eINSTANCE;
    EPackage ePackage = ecore.createEPackage();
    ePackage.setName(vocabulary.getName());
    ePackage.setNsURI(vocabulary.getNamespace());
    ePackage.setNsPrefix(vocabulary.getName());

    Map<Class, EClass> classes = new HashMap<>();
    for (Classifier classifier: vocabulary.getClassifiers()) {
        if (classifier instanceof Class type) {
            EClass eClass = ecore.createEClass();
            eClass.setName(type.getName());
            ePackage.getEClassifiers().add(eClass);
            classes.put(type, eClass);
        }
    }
    classes.forEach((type, eClass) -> {
        for (Feature feature: type.getFeatures()) {
            if (feature instanceof Attribute attribute
                    && attribute.getType() instanceof PrimitiveTypeReference primitive) {
                EAttribute eAttribute = ecore.createEAttribute();
                eAttribute.setName(attribute.getName());
                eAttribute.setEType(dataType(primitive.getKind()));
                eAttribute.setLowerBound(attribute.getLowerBound());
                eAttribute.setUpperBound(attribute.getUpperBound());
                eAttribute.setID(attribute.isIdentifying());
                eClass.getEStructuralFeatures().add(eAttribute);
            } else if (feature instanceof Reference reference
                    && reference.getType() instanceof ClassifierReference target) {
                EReference eReference = ecore.createEReference();
                eReference.setName(reference.getName());
                eReference.setEType(classes.get((Class) target.getTarget()));
                eReference.setContainment(reference.isContainment());
                eReference.setLowerBound(reference.getLowerBound());
                eReference.setUpperBound(reference.getUpperBound());
                eClass.getEStructuralFeatures().add(eReference);
            }
        }
    });
    new ResourceImpl(URI.createURI(ePackage.getNsURI())).getContents().add(ePackage);
    return ePackage;
}

private static EDataType dataType(PrimitiveKind kind) {
    return switch (kind) {
        case NUMBER -> EcorePackage.Literals.EDOUBLE;
        case BOOLEAN -> EcorePackage.Literals.EBOOLEAN;
        case DATE, DATE_TIME -> EcorePackage.Literals.EDATE;
        default -> EcorePackage.Literals.ESTRING;
    };
}

9.3. What a projection decides

A projection decides what the neutral model leaves open:

  • whether a Class becomes an interface, a type alias or a class;

  • how a DataType is represented, from its representation;

  • what to do with what the target cannot say: wildcards, unions in Java, operations in a data schema;

  • which annotations it reads, by their source.

The model stays the same for every projection, which is what lets one vocabulary serve many.

Chapter 10. Instances whose type is a reference

10.1. The rule that keeps it small

An operation belongs to Object only if a consumer cannot compute it from the others and the type without loading data it did not ask for. Contents are derivable from the type’s containment references and get, so there is no operation for contents. size and the ranged get pass the rule, because they answer without fetching every value, and the ranged get is what makes a large model usable over a network. isResolved is the one concession: references cross resources by URI, and something has to represent "not here yet".

10.2. Classified later

Because the type is a reference, an object can exist before it is classified, and be classified later. That is the shape elicitation has: a thing is named before anyone knows what kind of thing it is, refinement proceeds by dividing, an agent proposes a classification and a person corrects it. Before classification, values wait in annotations; classification promotes what fits into features and leaves the rest where it was.

  • A user interface over anything Meta describes, with no generated code.

  • Queries over instances and their types in one statement, because the type is an edge like any other.

Chapter 11. AI-assisted domain design

The people who know what the words of a domain mean are rarely the people who write them in a precise notation. A product manager at a bank can spot a wrong definition of a joint account in a second, and has no reason to learn a grammar. The formats of Chapter 12 will meet such people in the spreadsheets and diagrams they already use PLANNED. An AI assistant can meet them today: they talk, or hand over what they have, the assistant writes the text syntax, the build checks it, and they correct what it says about their business.

This chapter is written first for those subject-matter experts, and needs no code as far as Section 11.5. From there on, it is for the person who sets the project up.

11.1. Who checks what

An assistant is good at the mechanical part of modeling: turning a description, a glossary, a spreadsheet extract or meeting notes into structure. Correcting a draft is much cheaper than writing one from nothing. But an assistant writes a plausible definition of a term it has never heard with the same ease as a correct one, and a plausible definition can get past a reader who does not know the domain. So review only works where the reviewer can judge what is reviewed, and this way of working gives each check to whoever can:

Who Judges Cannot judge

The assistant

Nothing final. It drafts, and it fixes what the validator reports

What the words mean here, unless someone told it

The validator, in the build

Whether the file is well formed: the syntax, that every type exists, that opposites and keys exist, that imports within the project resolve

Whether a definition is right, or a rule is true

The subject-matter expert

Whether the definitions and the rules are true of the business

Whether the file is well formed, and does not have to

A developer or an architect

How the vocabulary fits with others: its namespace, what other teams already own, whether a change is safe to publish

What the business means by its words

The structure and the meaning are the two things a vocabulary can get wrong, and they fail in different hands. A vocabulary the validator accepts can still be wrong about the business, and it is the expert who decides that it is not.

11.2. How it goes

  1. Bring what exists. A glossary, a spreadsheet extract, a policy document, meeting notes, or your own words. An assistant works from what it is given, so the more of your organization’s own language it sees, the less it invents.

  2. Answer what it cannot infer. It asks what the vocabulary’s namespace is, a name that identifies it and belongs to your organization, and which terms other teams already own.

  3. It drafts. One file per package, with a definition for every term, written for a business reader.

  4. The build checks the draft. The assistant runs the validator, reads what it reports, and fixes every error at its line and column. You do not have to look at any of this. It tells you about warnings rather than hiding them.

  5. It tells you what it did, in terms of the business: the terms added, renamed or redefined, and anything it was unsure of.

  6. It reads the rules back, as plain statements without the notation, the ones it guessed first, and points you to a glossary the build has written.

  7. You correct it, in your own words. "An account can’t be without a holder." "We don’t say client, we say customer." It changes the file, and the build checks it again.

  8. The change is reviewed like any other. The vocabulary is a file in Git, so the change is a pull request: a developer or an architect looks after the structure, and you approve the meaning.

11.3. Reading a vocabulary without learning the notation

The definitions are prose, and you can read them as they are. The rules are in the structure, where they are easy to miss, and they are often the part an assistant guessed, because notes and glossaries rarely say whether an account can have no holder. You get them in two ways, and neither asks you to read the notation.

The first is the conversation. The skill has the assistant read the rules back after every draft or change, and each statement is a question only you can answer. From the bank’s vocabulary (Chapter 8):

In the file What to ask yourself

refers Customer[+] holders

The file says an account has at least one holder. Can an account ever have none, say after its only holder dies?

contains Statement[*] statements

The file says a statement is part of its account, and does not exist without it. Do you have to keep statements after an account is closed?

refers Product[1] product

The file says an account has exactly one product, which has a life of its own. Can an account move to another product?

CurrencyCode[1] currency

The file says an account is in exactly one currency. Is there a multi-currency account?

id string[1] accountNumber

The file says the number identifies the account. Is a number ever reused?

A wrong answer here is a correction to one line, made today. The same wrong answer found in a generated schema, a year from now, is a migration.

An assistant without the skill can be asked for the same thing:

Read this vocabulary back to me as plain statements of business rules, one for
each relationship and each required attribute. Mark the ones you guessed, and
ask me about those first.

The second is the glossary, which is how to check a whole vocabulary rather than the part that just changed. Each build without errors writes one, and since a program writes it from the file, it does not depend on what an assistant remembered to say. The Rule column states the structure in plain words:

A party the bank has a relationship with, through at least one account.

| Term | Type | Rule | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| customerNumber | text | required, identifies it | Identifies the customer, and is printed on statements. |

The validator accepts this, and I left it in the bank’s vocabulary because it is the kind of thing only you can settle. 11.4. What to watch for

The validator accepts every one of these, which is why they are yours to catch:

  • A definition that could be about anyone’s business. "The account of the customer" says nothing. Ask whether someone in your organization could disagree with it. If not, it is not yet a definition.

  • A rule stated more firmly than your sources do. The notes said "usually one"; the file says exactly one.

  • One word for two things, or two words for one. The sources say client and customer. An assistant may keep both, or merge them, without asking, and either could be wrong. Whichever you decide, it belongs in the definitions.

  • A term another team owns, copied instead of imported. Copies drift. A developer or an architect usually spots this one in review.

What you give an assistant goes wherever that assistant sends it, so check what your organization allows before you paste anything in. A vocabulary needs definitions, not records: it describes what a customer is, and no customer’s data has to be part of the conversation.

The conventions are the part a language reference would not give an assistant:

  • Every class, data type, enumeration and feature has a definition for a business reader, starting with one sentence that could stand alone in a glossary. Not how it is stored, and not "the X of the Y".

  • The multiplicity says the business rule, contains means the target does not exist without its owner, and two-way relationships are paired.

  • Money and identifiers are data types, not numbers, so that no projection turns money into a floating-point number.

  • A term another team owns is imported by namespace, not copied, and a namespace is never invented under someone else’s domain.

The model’s tests, SkillTests, check what an assistant learns from the skill: its examples are the files of this book, they validate, every nmeta block in its Markdown loads without errors, its read-back examples are lines of bank.nmeta, and the glossary is where it says. They do not test an assistant. What it writes is checked by the validator and by the people who own the words.

Chapter 12. Formats

A vocabulary is one model, and formats are how people put it in and get it out. The text syntax is the first format of this edition. The others below have been proven in Nasdanika’s work outside the SDK, and will be migrated to the runtime as vocabularies need them PLANNED.

The rule that makes many formats affordable: every format reads into Meta and every output is projected from Meta. Nothing converts from one format to another directly, so a new input format works with every output there is, and a new output with every input.

12.2. Hybrid authoring

Formats can also share one package PLANNED. Entities and relationships go on a diagram, as boxes and connections, where their shape is seen; attributes go in a spreadsheet or Markdown, where there is room for many; and descriptions go wherever prose is easiest to write.

The diagram is the backbone, and the other files are found from it, in one of two ways:

  • Implied, from the identifier or the path of a diagram element. The attributes of a box Customer on the page Retail are the sheet Customer of a workbook next to the diagram, or the table in Retail/Customer.md.

  • Explicit, in a property of the diagram element, such as a workbook and a sheet, or a Markdown file, which wins over what is implied.

Descriptions follow the same principle. An element’s description is the text on the diagram, such as a tooltip; or a file at the element’s path, found by trying extensions in order, Retail/Customer.md, then .adoc, then .html; or a file named in a property of the element. A description in a file becomes the element’s docRef in the core model, a document resolved relative to the model and rendered in its format, so it is not copied into the model at all.

For the bank, a hybrid package could look like this:

bank/

Chapter 13. Looking ahead

Formats come first, in Chapter 12. What follows are transformations of vocabularies, from Meta to Meta, and the dimensions a vocabulary varies in.

13.1. Flattening

Inheritance is how a model is authored. It is not always how it is consumed: a TypeScript interface, a JSON Schema for a tool call and a summary handed to an agent all read better as a flat class, with every inherited feature and operation inlined.

Flattening is a transformation from Meta to Meta PLANNED, not a trick inside each emitter: the input is a package, the output is a package whose classes declare everything they inherited, and every copied feature carries a marker back to the class that declared it. It is testable by comparison, reusable by every projection, and auditable.

Flattening is also where composition stops being free:

  • Most encounters of the same name are not clashes. A feature reached twice through a diamond is one feature, and an operation declared again with the same signature is an override. Deduplicate by identity, not by name.

  • The same name with different declarations is a clash, resolved by renaming, deterministically: the most derived declaration keeps the name, and the rename is recorded on the feature.

  • Renaming happens in Meta, not downstream, because TypeScript refuses an interface that extends two interfaces declaring a member incompatibly, and a strict tool schema has no room for the composition that would otherwise carry it.

What a cut must not do is erase distinctions: removing an ancestor’s name is recoverable by widening the cut, while replacing a class by its ancestor removes a distinction nothing can recover.

13.2. Variability

One vocabulary rarely means the same thing everywhere. A bank in the United States and Canada has two currencies, several jurisdictions, French as a legal requirement for consumer contracts in Quebec and a customer’s preference elsewhere, and state rules, such as California’s privacy law, over federal ones. Jurisdiction, currency, language and legal entity do not line up: Canadian customers commonly hold accounts in US dollars, so currency is not jurisdiction.

A fork per variant drifts within a release. Instead, classes, features and literals will be situated in coordinates, such as jurisdiction, language, time, product tier and audience PLANNED. One vocabulary then carries its variants together, the elements that differ marked with where and when they apply, and generating for a coordinate emits what applies there and nothing else: a product line, not a fork. Two operations will do it, and they are different on purpose:

  • Restriction drops what does not apply to a region and keeps the vocabulary variable in every dimension it still spans. It is set restriction, so restricting to Ontario and then adding Quebec gives what restricting to both would.

  • Specialization resolves a vocabulary at a point, such as Quebec, in French, as of a date, into a smaller vocabulary for one audience: the glossary a Quebec branch prints, or the schema a provincial integration validates against.

A resolved vocabulary records the point it was resolved at, so that a PDF says it is the Quebec French edition as of a date instead of merely being one, and can be compared with a sibling variant or resolved further.

13.3. Occupancy

Variability needs a way to say "this is true here, then, in that range" once, not five times: for jurisdictions, periods, versions, languages and product tiers alike. That is occupancy PLANNED. An element occupies a region of a space of dimensions, the region being a union of boxes, each a point or a range per dimension, and a dimension that is not mentioned is unconstrained, not unknown. Time is one dimension among others, with the best tooling and no special treatment.

The text syntax is ready for it. A facet attaches an occupancy to any element:

/** In Quebec, consumer contracts are in French unless the customer asks otherwise. */
@occupancy.Presence { jurisdiction: "CA-QC" }
string contractLanguage = "fr"

The bank’s vocabulary in Chapter 8 says exactly that today, and the reader records the facet as an annotation, with a warning that its type is not available. When the occupancy model is, the same text is checked against it, and restriction and specialization become transformations from Meta to Meta, testable by comparison like flattening.

13.4. Kinds and types

A type, a Meta class or an Xcore class, fixes structure: which features an element has. The runtime’s core model is gaining kinds, which say what an element is for regardless of its type. Generated Meta documentation will explain the difference, and the kind model comes first PLANNED.

The bank’s vocabulary already leans that way: a checking account product is an instance of Product, not a class, so a jurisdiction’s variant of a product is data, not a change to the vocabulary.

A loader that reads a vocabulary from where it is already written down, such as a database schema or an XML Schema.

Hybrid authoring

One vocabulary in several formats: entities and relationships on a diagram, attributes in a spreadsheet or Markdown, descriptions in files found from the diagram.

Identifying attribute

An attribute whose value identifies an instance, and which references may cite.

Metacircular

Able to describe itself: Meta’s package of classes can be written in Meta.

Occupancy

Where and when an element applies: a region of a space of dimensions, such as jurisdiction, time and language.

Projection

A transformation of a vocabulary into a form a consumer needs: a glossary, an Ecore package, TypeScript, a JSON Schema.

Read-back

Plain statements of the rules a vocabulary’s structure holds, which an assistant gives the people who own the words, the guessed ones first, so that they can correct them.

Published language

In Domain-Driven Design, a documented, shared vocabulary that bounded contexts translate into and out of.

Subject-matter expert

Someone who knows what the words of a domain mean, and need not know any notation: the person who judges a vocabulary’s definitions and rules.

Text syntax

Meta’s own notation, in .nmeta files: metamodels written the way Xcore writes Ecore models.

Validator

MetaValidator, which checks the .nmeta files of a directory together, and reports problems as a compiler does. It checks structure, not meaning.

Variability

How a vocabulary differs by jurisdiction, language, time or audience, carried in one vocabulary rather than in forks.

Vocabulary

A Meta package: the terms of a domain, their definitions, types and relations.
