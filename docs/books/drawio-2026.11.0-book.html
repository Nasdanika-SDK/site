Preface

This book is about diagrams that people draw and programs read. Most diagrams in an organization are drawn once, looked at a few times and then left to drift away from what they describe. They do not have to be. A diagram drawn in Draw.io is a structured document, and with a little discipline it can be documentation, configuration, a program’s wiring or the source of a model, while it stays a picture that anyone can open and change.

The first part of the book is the why: what diagrams are for, how much structure a particular diagram should carry, and why Draw.io is a good editor to build on. The second part is the how, for Java developers: the Draw.io module of the Nasdanika SDK Runtime, which reads, navigates, changes and writes Draw.io documents. The third part says what is coming next.

Who this book is for

People who draw. Architects, analysts and engineers who already use Draw.io, and who would like their diagrams to do more than sit in a wiki. Part one is for you, and needs no code.

Java developers who are handed diagrams and asked to do something with them: check them, generate from them, publish them, or keep them in step with a system. Read the book in order; part two assumes part one.

Anyone who builds tools on diagrams. The module is small and has few dependencies, and its document model mirrors Draw.io’s file format closely enough that what you learn here holds for the format itself.

Part I: The Why

What diagrams are for, how much structure a diagram should carry, and why Draw.io is the editor this book builds on. No code is needed to read this part.

Chapter 1. Diagrams are communication

Diagrams are older than writing. People drew maps, tallies and kinship long before they wrote sentences, and they still reach for a whiteboard when a conversation gets complicated. You might expect such an old form of communication to be well understood. In my experience it is not: there is a gap in how people approach diagramming, and it costs organizations a great deal of time.

Like writing, diagramming is not an innate skill. Being able to connect two boxes with an arrow does not make someone a good diagrammer, any more than being able to write a sentence makes someone a good writer. And I’ve seen many diagrams where people can’t even connect two boxes correctly - they draw arrows between boxes, but the arrows do not connect to the boxes - so there is no relationship. This part of the book is about thinking in diagrams: what a diagram is for, who it is for, and how much structure it should carry.

1.1. What a diagram is

A diagram is a symbolic representation of information, and in the sense this book uses, a particular genre of it: shapes connected by lines, arrows or other visual links, which show things and their relationships. I extend the genre to quantitative information too, because the size, position and color of a shape can carry numbers as well as categories (Chapter 3).

Diagrams fall into two broad groups.

Representations only. The diagram leaves it to the beholder to work out what a shape means. A blue rounded rectangle might be a system, a team or a deployment unit, and only a legend, a convention or a conversation says which. Draw.io, Visio and PowerPoint produce diagrams of this kind.

Representations in a domain language. UML, BPMN, ArchiMate and SysML define what each shape means, so a reader who knows the language reads the diagram without help.

Enterprise IT is dominated by the first kind, and Draw.io and PowerPoint are its common formats. UML has been around for about thirty years and is still used narrowly and often misused. Simon Brown, the author of the C4 model, made the same observation: many teams have abandoned the modeling languages for simpler boxes-and-lines diagrams, and in doing so have lost some of their ability to communicate visually.

I think the reason is that every enterprise has a language of its own, rarely written down. It speaks of products, channels, platforms, capabilities, controls and owners, with meanings that are local to it. It does not speak UML or BPMN, and a tool that imposes either one is resisted. Tools that make it easy to build a specialized diagram editor for such a language, Eclipse Sirius among them, are not widely used either, because a specialized editor is one more tool to install, learn and keep.

What remains is the general-purpose editor and the boxes-and-lines diagram, which everybody can draw and nobody can check.

1.2. Who a diagram is for

A diagram is a message, and a message has readers. I find it useful to name four of them.

Yourself, now. You draw to understand the problem in front of you and to break it into pieces. Such a diagram may never be shown to anyone, and that is fine.

Yourself, later. The faintest ink is better than the strongest memory. What is obvious today will not be obvious in a year, when you come back to a decision and need to know why it was made.

Other people. You draw to explain an intent, to gather feedback, or to instruct. This is where most diagrams are aimed, and where most of their cost lies: every time a reader has to ask the author what a shape means, the diagram has failed a little.

Computers. You draw to tell a program what to do: to configure it, to generate code or documentation from the diagram, or to execute it. And, increasingly, AI assistants, which read diagrams as context, summarize them for people, or let people ask questions of them. An assistant is a reader that does not get tired of asking what a shape means, as long as the diagram has an answer.

1.3. The cost of a diagram

A diagram has two costs. The production cost is the effort of drawing it and keeping it current. The consumption cost is the effort its readers spend to get what they need from it: to find it, to understand it, to ask about what they do not understand, and to act on it.

I call a diagram efficient when it is cheap to produce and effective when it is cheap to consume. The two pull against each other. A sketch is cheap to draw and costly to read, because everything not on the page has to be explained in person. A diagram with documented elements, links between levels of detail and properties that programs can check is more work to draw and much less work to read.

The right balance depends on the readers. A whiteboard sketch for a one-hour meeting should stay a sketch. An architecture diagram that a hundred people consult over three years deserves documentation, and one that drives a deployment deserves properties that a program can check. The goal is the least total effort to communicate, production and consumption together.

1.4. What I wanted from a diagram

For years I wanted a way of working with diagrams that would let me:

  • create a diagram in an editor that everyone can use;

  • share it with people who do not have that editor, in a browser;

  • document its elements with rich text, beyond what a tooltip holds;

  • give its elements sub-diagrams, nested as deeply as needed;

  • read it from a program, as data;

  • associate behavior with its elements, so that the diagram is executable;

  • and map its elements to a model with a defined meaning, when a defined meaning is what the readers need.

Each of these is a point on a continuum, from a bare picture to a model. The next chapter walks along it.

Chapter 2. The visual communication continuum

A diagram does not have to be either a picture or a model. Between the two lies a continuum of small steps, and each step adds a little structure to the same file. A diagram can stop at any point and move further later, when its readers need more, without being redrawn in another tool.

2.1. Picture

Shapes, lines and labels. The meaning is in the heads of the people who drew it and of those who were in the room.

A picture is the right place to stop more often than people think. It is cheap to draw, it gets the problem out of one head and onto a page, and it is often thrown away after the meeting it was drawn for. Its consumption cost is high, but if it has few readers, that cost is never paid.

2.2. Interactive diagram

The same picture, with a little more of the author’s knowledge put into it:

  • tooltips on elements, so a reader can hover to see what a shape is;

  • links from an element to another page of the diagram, for the next level of detail, or to a page elsewhere;

  • layers and tags, so that one diagram shows several levels of detail, or several concerns, and a reader or a presenter turns them on and off;

  • a viewer in the browser, so that anyone can read the diagram, click through its pages and toggle its layers without a Draw.io installation.

All of this is plain Draw.io. What the module adds at this point is the ability to do it from code: to publish a diagram as a page with the viewer embedded (Chapter 5), and to check that its links resolve (Chapter 10). AVAILABLE

2.3. Documented diagram

Elements get documentation: a paragraph, a page, a page with sub-diagrams. A documentation site is generated from the diagram, with a page per documented element, a navigation tree that follows the containment and the links of the diagram, and search over the text of the pages and of the diagrams themselves.

This is the first point where the consumption cost drops sharply. A reader no longer has to ask the author what an element is; it has a page. The author documents what matters now and leaves the rest for later, for others, or for never. An element without documentation still appears on the diagram; it simply has no page.

Nasdanika core generates such sites from Draw.io diagrams today, and the Internet Banking System demo is one. In the runtime, site generation is PLANNED (Chapter 14).

2.4. Diagram as data

Elements get properties with agreed meanings: an owner, a type, a protocol, a criticality, a cost-center. Draw.io stores them with the element and shows them in its Edit Data dialog. A program can then read the diagram as data:

  • to check it: every system has an owner, no customer-facing system talks to the mainframe directly, every connection has a protocol;

  • to report from it: the systems each team owns, the connections that cross a trust boundary;

  • to configure something with it: a diagram of a deployment, a pipeline or an integration, from which a program reads its settings;

  • to change it: color the systems that are past their retirement date, add a tag to every element a change touches, and save the diagram for people to see.

This is the point the module is built for, and most of part two is about it. AVAILABLE

2.5. Executable diagram

Behavior is bound to elements. A property names the code that an element stands for, a processor is created for each element, and processors talk to each other along the connections of the diagram. The diagram is the program’s wiring, and someone who can draw can rewire it.

Nasdanika core has a framework for executable graphs and diagrams, with processors, handlers and endpoints wired along connections, synchronously or concurrently. In the runtime, it is PLANNED.

2.6. Diagram as a model

Elements are mapped to the objects of a typed model, with a metamodel that defines what a system, a team or a capability is, which properties each has, and how they relate. The diagram becomes one of the sources of that model. Everything a model gives is then available: validation against the metamodel, references across diagrams, spreadsheets and code, generation, and tools that understand the model without knowing that it was drawn.

This is the point where the enterprise’s own language gets written down, as a metamodel, while the people who speak it keep drawing in the editor they know. In the runtime, the first half of this point is built, outside this module: the runtime’s Draw.io model loads a diagram into a resource set as a model, and its elements, as elements of the runtime’s diagram model, can depict the objects of any other model (Chapter 14). Mapping them by properties alone, without code, is PLANNED.

2.7. Choosing a point

Every step to the right costs the author more and the readers less. A few rules of thumb have served me well:

  • Start on the left. Draw the picture first. Structure added before anyone has looked at the diagram is often structure nobody needed.

  • Move right when readers ask the same question twice. The second "what is this?" is a tooltip, the second "where are the details?" is a link or a page, and the second "who owns this?" is a property.

  • Move right when a program needs the answer. As soon as a check, a report or a deployment depends on what the diagram says, the diagram is data, whether or not anyone admits it. Better to admit it and give its properties names.

  • Never redraw to move. Each point keeps the file of the point before it. A documented diagram is still a picture, and a diagram mapped to a model can still be opened, read and changed by someone who has never heard of the model.

The rest of this book is about the middle of the continuum: diagrams as data, read and written from Java.

Chapter 3. What a diagram is made of

Before Draw.io, a look at diagrams in general: what they are made of, whatever the tool, paper and pencil included. Most of the API in part two is a Draw.io-specific way of reaching these few concepts.

3.1. Nodes, connections and what they stand for

Diagrams show things and their relationships with shapes joined by lines. I call the shapes nodes, the lines connections, and the things they stand for entities. Entities have information associated with them, which I call features, and they can do things, which I call operations.

Think of entities as nouns, features as adjectives and operations as verbs. In "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog", the fox and the dog are entities; quick, brown and lazy are features; jumps over is an operation, perhaps with "over" as its argument.

A diagram contains diagram elements, nodes and connections, and describes a domain, a collection of entities with their features and operations. A few facts about this structure matter throughout the book:

  • A node can contain other diagram elements. A system contains its containers, a container its components.

  • A connection joins a source node to a target node, but either end can be missing. A dangling connection is legitimate: an arrow from a system to "somewhere we have not decided yet".

  • A diagram element can stand for one entity, for a group of entities, for a feature or an operation, or for nothing at all. A frame, a legend or a decorative line is a purely visual element.

  • The relationship between an element and what it stands for is not in the picture. It is established by the shape, the label, the tooltip, a link, a property, and the shared understanding of the people who read it.

Features come in two kinds. Attributes have scalar values, or collections of them: a name, a color, an age. References point to other entities: a person’s employer, the systems a team owns. On a diagram, a reference is often a connection, and sometimes containment: the teams inside a department, the containers inside a system.

3.2. Visual features

Diagram elements have features of their own, apart from what they stand for, and the visual ones can carry information as surely as a label. Most diagram editors support visual features only, and Draw.io adds non-visual ones, properties, which Chapter 8 is about. Each visual feature below can encode both categories and quantities.

Label. Text on an element or a connection, plain or formatted. In Draw.io, labels are often HTML, which matters when a program reads them (Chapter 7).

Shape. Circle, rectangle, cylinder, cloud. Circles for apples and rectangles for oranges is a category; the ratio of a rectangle’s sides can be a quantity.

Icon. An image used instead of a shape. There are millions of icons, and Draw.io has libraries of thousands, from cloud providers to network equipment.

Border. Width, color and pattern: solid, dashed, dotted. In this book’s figures, a dashed border means planned.

Connection. Width, color and pattern, as for a border, and also decorations at both ends and labels along the line.

Size. Lines of code, account balance, transaction volume: the bigger the shape, the larger the number.

Geometry. Position and distance. Position can order nodes, left to right and top to bottom, or clockwise around a hub, and distance can carry a value. Chapter 12 turns geometry into order with comparators.

Color. Colors can be ordered, by hue on the HSV or HSB scale, or put in buckets, by distance to a set of base colors: blue for in progress and green for done, as in many issue trackers. Matching colors exactly is brittle; matching by least distance is not.

Opacity. A translucent element can be less important, or not available yet.

Gradient. A gradient between two colors can say that an element belongs to two categories.

Z-order. When shapes overlap, which one is on top can carry meaning, alone or together with position.

A diagram that uses visual features to carry information should say so, in a legend or in its documentation. A diagram that a program reads can do better: it can read those features, because in Draw.io every one of them is in the element’s style, which the API reads as a map (Chapter 11).

Chapter 4. Why Draw.io

There are many diagram editors. This book builds on Draw.io, also known as diagrams.net, for reasons that have little to do with drawing and much to do with what happens to a diagram after it is drawn.

4.1. What it gives you

Draw.io is a diagram editor written in HTML5 and JavaScript, developed by JGraph, open source under the Apache License 2.0, and free.

  • It runs everywhere. In the browser, as a desktop application for Windows, macOS and Linux, inside Confluence and Visual Studio Code, and self-hosted as a web application or a container.

  • It stores diagrams where you already keep files. The local disk, Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox, GitHub and GitLab. A diagram is a file, versioned and reviewed like any other.

  • It has a large library of shapes, more than two thousand, and accepts images pasted from anywhere.

  • It has a viewer. A diagram embedded in a web page can be clicked, zoomed, shown full screen, its layers and tags toggled, and opened in the editor, without a Draw.io installation.

  • Elements have tooltips, editable IDs and properties, in the Edit Data dialog, so an element can carry information that is not drawn.

  • A file has pages, and elements link to pages, so one file holds a hierarchy of diagrams, from a system context down to the code.

  • Layers and tags show and hide elements in groups, so one diagram serves several audiences or reveals its details step by step in a presentation.

  • Placeholders put property values into labels and tooltips.

  • Libraries and templates make reusable sets of elements, preconfigured with names, tooltips and links, the beginning of a diagramming ecosystem in an organization.

4.2. What it does not give you

What Draw.io lacks is what the rest of the continuum needs. A tooltip is too small for documentation. Links between pages stop at the file: you cannot link to an element, or to a page in another file. And there is no programmatic way, outside the browser, to read a diagram, to navigate it as a structure, to check it, or to change it.

That is what the module provides, and, in the runtime’s plans, what the points to its right build on (Chapter 14).

4.3. The file format

A Draw.io file is XML, and its structure is the structure the API exposes (Chapter 6):

  • mxfile, the document, contains one diagram element per page;

  • a diagram contains an mxGraphModel, with the page’s settings;

  • the model contains a root, which contains mxCell elements: a root cell, the layers, and the nodes and connections of each layer, each with a parent attribute;

  • a cell with properties is wrapped in an object element, which holds the properties, the label, the tooltip and the link as attributes;

  • a cell’s style attribute holds its visual features as key=value; pairs, and an mxGeometry child holds its position, size and the points of a connection.

The content of a page may be compressed: deflated, Base64-encoded text inside the diagram element, which is how the desktop application saves by default. Compressed files are smaller; uncompressed ones can be read, diffed and merged. The API reads both and writes either (Chapter 5).

4.4. Diagrams in PNG images

Draw.io can save a diagram inside a PNG image: "Include a copy of my diagram" on export, or a file named .drawio.png, which the editor opens and saves directly. The result is one file that is an ordinary image to everything that shows images, and a diagram to Draw.io.

Every figure in this book is such a file. A figure cannot drift away from its source, because the figure is the source. The module reads the diagram out of the image, so the book’s tests load the very figures you see, and a listing that works on a figure works on what the figure shows.

Two notes for your own diagrams:

  • Export above 100 percent. A PNG is raster. A diagram exported at 1x looks soft in print and on high-density screens; this book’s figures are rendered at 2x.

  • Prefer PNG to SVG for documents built with Asciidoctor PDF. Draw.io can embed a diagram in SVG as well, but it writes formatted labels as HTML inside foreignObject elements, which Asciidoctor PDF does not draw.

4.5. The module

The module is a salvage of the Draw.io API that Nasdanika core has had for years. It keeps the API for reading, navigating, changing and writing documents, and leaves out what tied it to the rest of core: graph processing, the Ecore model of Draw.io documents, which the runtime has rebuilt as a module of its own, and Spring Expression Language (Appendix B).

It is small, and it depends on little: the JDK’s XML and image APIs, four small libraries for JSON, HTML, text and encoding, and the runtime’s common module, which brings EMF’s core libraries and the OpenTelemetry API. You can use it without the rest of the runtime.

Chapter 5. Getting started

5.3. Loading a diagram

A PNG image can embed more than one diagram, so Document.loadFromPngMetadata returns a list. The tests load the figures with a small helper:

Listing 3. Loading a diagram embedded in a PNG image (Diagrams.java)
/**
 * Loads the diagram embedded in a PNG image next to this class.
 */
public static Document load(String name) throws Exception {
    URL resource = Diagrams.class.getResource(name);
    List<Document> documents = Document.loadFromPngMetadata(resource); // (1)
    if (documents.size() != 1) {
        throw new IllegalArgumentException(name + " embeds " + documents.size() + " diagrams");

    return documents.getFirst();
}

  1. loadFromPngMetadata takes a URL, or an InputStream with the URI of the image, and optionally a Context (Chapter 8).

5.4. Saving and loading

save writes the document as XML. Its argument decides compression: false writes pages as plain XML, true compresses them, and null keeps each document as it was loaded.

Listing 5. Saving a diagram as a .drawio file and loading it back
Path path = Path.of("target", "book", "internet-banking.drawio");
Files.createDirectories(path.getParent());
Files.writeString(path, document.save(false)); // (1)

Document reloaded = Document.load(path.toFile()); // (2)
assertThat(reloaded.getPages()).hasSize(2);
assertThat(reloaded.getURI().lastSegment()).isEqualTo("internet-banking.drawio");

  1. Uncompressed, indented XML, which reads well in a diff and in a code review.

  2. The document remembers where it was loaded from. Relative links to other documents are resolved against that URI (Chapter 10).

A compressed document is smaller and opaque. Loading from a string needs a base URI only if the document links to other documents:

Listing 6. Compressed pages, and loading from a string
String compressed = document.save(true);
assertThat(compressed).doesNotContain("mxGraphModel"); // (1)
Document fromString = Document.load(compressed, null); // (2)
assertThat(fromString.getPageById("containers").getName()).isEqualTo("Containers");

  1. The pages are deflated and Base64-encoded, so the XML of the model is not visible.

  2. The URI is null: the document has no location of its own.

The API loads documents from every source Java has. Document.create makes an empty one, which Chapter 11 fills:

Listing 7. Every way to get a document
File file = new File(
    "src/test/resources/org/nasdanika/sdk/runtime/drawio/tests/compressed.drawio");
Document fromFile = Document.load(file);
Document fromUrl = Document.load(file.toURI().toURL());
Document fromUri = Document.load(URI.createFileURI(file.getAbsolutePath()));
Document fromStream;
try (var in = Files.newInputStream(file.toPath())) {
    fromStream = Document.load(in, URI.createFileURI(file.getAbsolutePath()));

Document empty = Document.create(false, null);

Each load method also takes a Context, which supplies properties, filters them, and opens the streams of linked documents (Chapter 8, Chapter 10). Loading from a URL or a File goes through a cache of documents, so documents that link to each other are loaded once each.

  2. Document.isDataURI tells such a URI apart from a location.

Chapter 6. The document model

The API is a set of interfaces over the XML of a Draw.io file, one per part of it, implemented in the impl package. This chapter is the map; the chapters after it use it.

Figure 3. The document model. Open arrows are references, named after their getters; closed arrows are generalizations.

6.1. From document to element

Document is the file, the mxfile element. It has a list of pages, creates new ones, finds a page by name or by ID, saves itself, and loads documents that it links to.

Page is a diagram element: a name, an ID and a model. A page is also where tags live.

Model is the page’s mxGraphModel: the grid, the background, the shadow and the page view. It has one Root, the root cell, which contains the layers. For most purposes a page, its model and its root are one thing; the API keeps them apart because the format does.

Layer contains the elements of a page. Every page has at least one layer; in Draw.io, the Layers panel shows them.

LayerElement is what a layer contains: a node or a connection.

Node is a shape. A node is also a layer: it contains nodes and connections of its own, which is how a system contains its containers. It has a geometry and connection points, and it is Connectable: connections start and end at nodes.

Connection is a line from a source to a target, either of which can be missing. It has points along its path and a node of its own for each extra label.

ConnectionPoint is a fixed point on the outline of a node where a connection leaves or arrives, and is connectable too.

Tag is a name that elements of a page share, so that they can be shown and hidden together.

The figure leaves out three interfaces that the others extend. Element is the base of everything above: it has a URI, a marker that says where it came from in its file, the DOM element behind it, and the visitor and stream methods of Chapter 7. ModelElement is the base of the root, layers, nodes and connections: an ID, a label, a tooltip, a link, a style, properties, tags and visibility, and the selectors of Chapter 9. LinkTarget marks what a link can point to: a page or a model element (Chapter 10).

6.2. The figure checks the API

The figure is a diagram that a test reads. Its node labels are interface names, its open arrows are labeled with the names of getters, and its closed arrows have a relationship property of extends. The test checks the figure against the API with reflection, so a renamed interface or getter fails the build of this book until the figure is redrawn:

Listing 10. Checking the figure against the API (MetamodelTests.java)
@Test
public void testFigureMatchesApi() throws Exception {
    Document metamodel = Diagrams.load("metamodel.png");
    List<Node> types = metamodel
        .stream()
        .filter(Node.class::isInstance)
        .map(Node.class::cast)
        .toList();
    assertThat(types).hasSize(11);
    for (Node type: types) {
        assertThat(apiType(type).isInterface()).as(type.getLabel()).isTrue(); // (1)
    }

    List<Connection> relationships = metamodel
        .stream()
        .filter(Connection.class::isInstance)
        .map(Connection.class::cast)
        .toList();
    for (Connection relationship: relationships) {
        Class<?> source = apiType((Node) relationship.getSource());
        Class<?> target = apiType((Node) relationship.getTarget());
        switch (relationship.getProperty("relationship")) {
        case "extends" -> assertThat(target.isAssignableFrom(source)) // (2)
            .as(source.getSimpleName() + " extends " + target.getSimpleName())
            .isTrue();
        case "reference" -> {
            String name = relationship.getLabel();
            String getter = "get" + Character.toUpperCase(name.charAt(0)) + name.substring(1);
            Method method = source.getMethod(getter); // (3)
            assertThat(method.getGenericReturnType().getTypeName())
                .as(source.getSimpleName() + "." + getter + "()")
                .contains(target.getSimpleName());
        }
        default -> throw new AssertionError("Unknown relationship " + relationship);
        }
    }
}

private static Class<?> apiType(Node node) throws ClassNotFoundException {
    return Class.forName(Document.class.getPackageName() + "." + node.getLabel());
}

  1. Every node names an interface of the API’s package.

  2. A generalization arrow is an extends: the source type is assignable to the target type.

  3. A reference arrow is a getter of its source whose return type mentions its target. getPages() returns List<Page>, getTags() on a page returns Map<String, Tag>.

It is a small example of the continuum at work. The diagram is documentation for people and a specification for a test, and the test keeps the documentation honest.

6.3. Types and generics

The interfaces are generic in themselves: Element<E extends Element<E>>, ModelElement<M extends ModelElement<M>>, Layer<L extends Layer<L>>. The parameter lets methods such as style return the element’s own type, and in client code it is almost always a wildcard: ModelElement<?>, Layer<?>. Node and Connection are not generic, so code that works with them reads as plain Java.

A model element that is a node or a connection is a LayerElement, and you tell them apart with instanceof. Streams of a document are streams of Element<?>, filtered and cast to what a listing needs, as the next chapter shows.

getElement() returns the org.w3c.dom.Element behind any part of a diagram. It is there for what the API does not cover, such as attributes of the model that have no getter. Changes made through the DOM are saved like any others.

Chapter 7. Navigating a diagram

A program that reads a diagram walks it, searches it, follows its connections and folds it into a result. The API has a way to do each.

7.1. Walking the containment

Pages contain a model, the model a root, the root layers, and layers their elements. A loop over that structure is the most direct way to read a diagram:

Listing 11. Walking pages, layers and elements (NavigationTests.java)
List<String> outline = new ArrayList<>();
for (Page page: document.getPages()) {
    outline.add(page.getName());
    for (Layer<?> layer: page.getModel().getRoot().getLayers()) {
        outline.add("  layer " + layer.getId());
        for (LayerElement<?> element: layer.getElements()) {
            outline.add("    " + element.getId());
        }
    }
}
assertThat(outline).startsWith(
    "System Context",
    "  layer 1",
    "    customer",
    "    ibs",
    "    mainframe",
    "    email",
    "    uses-ibs");
assertThat(outline).contains("  layer notes", "    legend", "Containers", "    ibs-boundary");

The layer with ID 1 is the default layer that Draw.io creates on every page; the Notes layer of the running example has the ID notes. Elements come in the order of the file, which is the z-order of the page: what was drawn later is on top. getElements() returns the elements directly in a layer; a node’s own getElements() returns what it contains, because a node is a layer.

7.2. Streams

stream() returns an element and everything it contains, depth first, as a Stream<Element<?>>. On a document, that is every page, model, layer, node and connection in it. Filtering by type and property is the everyday way to find things:

Listing 12. Finding the software systems by a property
List<String> systems = document
    .stream()
    .filter(Node.class::isInstance)
    .map(Node.class::cast)
    .filter(node -> "software-system".equals(node.getProperty("type")))
    .map(Diagrams::text)
    .toList();
assertThat(systems).containsExactly(
    "Internet Banking System [Software System]",
    "Mainframe Banking System [Software System]",
    "E-mail System [Software System]");

Labels with html=1 in their style are HTML. The running example’s labels have a bold name and a line break before the C4 type, so the listing reads them through a helper that keeps the text:

Listing 13. The text of a label (Diagrams.java)
/**
 * Labels of elements with html=1 in their style are HTML. This is the text a reader sees.
 */
public static String text(ModelElement<?> element) {
    String label = element.getLabel();
    return label == null ? null : Jsoup.parse(label).text();
}

The helper uses jsoup, which the module requires as well; add it to your own project if you need it.

Two shortcuts search a whole document or page for a single element:

Listing 14. Finding an element by ID and by property
ModelElement<?> ibs = document.getModelElementById("ibs");
ModelElement<?> core = document.getModelElementByProperty("owner", "Core Banking");
assertThat(core.getId()).isEqualTo("mainframe");
assertThat(ibs.getModel().getPage().getName()).isEqualTo("System Context");

getModelElementsByProperty(name, value) returns all of them. IDs are unique within a page, not within a document; getModelElementById on a document returns the first match in page order. Draw.io generates IDs such as 7KSC1_O8d7ACaxm1iSCq-1, and lets you edit them: right-click, Edit Data, and click the ID. Readable IDs, as in the running example, make diagrams far easier to work with from code, and worth the minute they take.

7.3. Connections

A node knows the connections that start and end at it. getOutgoingConnections() and getIncomingConnections() return the ones attached to the node itself; getAllOutgoingConnections() and getAllIncomingConnections() add the ones attached to its connection points (Chapter 11).

Listing 15. Following connections
Node ibs = (Node) document.getModelElementById("ibs");
Map<String, String> uses = ibs
    .getOutgoingConnections()
    .stream()
    .collect(Collectors.toMap(
        Connection::getLabel,
        connection -> Util.getConnectableNode(connection.getTarget()).getId())); // (1)
assertThat(uses).containsOnly(
    Map.entry("Gets account information from and makes payments using", "mainframe"),
    Map.entry("Sends e-mail using", "email"));

assertThat(ibs.getIncomingConnections())
    .extracting(Connection::getId)
    .containsExactly("uses-ibs");

  1. A connection’s source and target are Connectable: a node or a connection point. Util.getConnectableNode returns the node in both cases.

7.4. Visitors

accept visits an element and its children bottom up: children first, then the element itself, with a map from each child to the result of visiting it. That makes a visitor a fold, in which every element computes its result from its children’s:

Listing 16. Counting nodes with a visitor
BiFunction<Element<?>, Map<? extends Element<?>, Integer>, Integer> counter =
    (element, childResults) -> {
        int nodes = element instanceof Node ? 1 : 0;
        for (Integer childResult: childResults.values()) { // (1)
            nodes += childResult;
        }
        return nodes;
    };
assertThat(systemContext.accept(counter)).isEqualTo(5); // (2)
assertThat(document.getPageByName("Containers").accept(counter)).isEqualTo(5);

  1. The results of the children. null results are left out of the map.

  2. Four nodes on the default layer and the note on the Notes layer.

accept also takes a Consumer<Element<?>> and a connection base, for visitors that collect rather than compute.

7.5. Connection base

Draw.io puts a connection in a layer, or in the node that contains both of its ends. For many purposes that parent is not the logical one. In a diagram of systems, a connection from the Internet Banking System to the mainframe is something the Internet Banking System does, so it belongs under its source. In a mind map, where arrows point from a topic to its parent, a connection belongs under its target.

ConnectionBase says which:

  • PARENT, the default, keeps connections where the file puts them;

  • SOURCE makes each connection a child of its source node;

  • TARGET makes each connection a child of its target node.

The connection base applies to visitors, to stream(ConnectionBase) and to URI resolution (Chapter 10):

Listing 17. The same page under two connection bases
BiFunction<Element<?>, Map<Element<?>, String>, String> tree =
    (element, childResults) -> {
        String children = String.join(", ", childResults.values());
        String id = element instanceof ModelElement<?> me ? me.getId() : "page";
        return children.isEmpty() ? id : id + "(" + children + ")";
    };
String byParent = systemContext.accept(tree, ConnectionBase.PARENT);
String bySource = systemContext.accept(tree, ConnectionBase.SOURCE); // (1)
assertThat(bySource).contains("ibs(ibs-mainframe, ibs-email)");
assertThat(bySource).contains("customer(uses-ibs)");
assertThat(byParent).doesNotContain("ibs(ibs-mainframe");

  1. Under SOURCE, connections leave the layer and appear under the nodes they start at.

7.6. Tags and layers

Tags are names in an element’s tags property, separated by spaces, and Draw.io’s Edit Tags dialog and the viewer’s tag filter work with them. A page collects the tags of its elements:

Listing 18. Tags of a page and of an element
assertThat(systemContext.getTags().keySet())
    .containsExactlyInAnyOrder("person", "internal", "legacy", "note"); // (1)

Tag legacy = systemContext.getTags().get("legacy");
assertThat(legacy.getElements())
    .extracting(ModelElement::getId)
    .containsExactly("mainframe");

ModelElement<?> mainframe = document.getModelElementById("mainframe");
assertThat(mainframe.getTags())
    .extracting(Tag::getName)
    .containsExactlyInAnyOrder("internal", "legacy");

  1. getTags() on a page is computed from its elements, so it has exactly the tags in use. createTag(name) makes a tag to add to an element’s getTags() set, which writes the tags property.

Layers have a label, which Draw.io’s Layers panel shows, and visibility. Hiding a layer hides it in the editor and in the viewer, which makes layers a way to keep notes, annotations or work in progress in a diagram without showing them:

Listing 19. Hiding a layer
Layer<?> notes = systemContext
    .getModel()
    .getRoot()
    .getLayers()
    .stream()
    .filter(layer -> "Notes".equals(layer.getLabel()))
    .findFirst()
    .orElseThrow();
assertThat(notes.isVisible()).isTrue();
notes.setVisible(false); // (1)
assertThat(document.save(false)).contains("visible=\"0\"");

  1. setVisible works on any model element, not only on layers. An invisible element is still in the document and still found by streams and visitors.

Chapter 8. Properties and placeholders

Properties are what turn a picture into data. In Draw.io, select an element, choose Edit Data from its context menu (Ctrl+M or Cmd+M), and add a property by name. The file stores it as an attribute of the element’s object element, next to the label, the tooltip and the link.

8.1. Reading properties

Listing 20. Properties of the Internet Banking System (PropertiesTests.java)
ModelElement<?> ibs = document.getModelElementById("ibs");
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("type")).isEqualTo("software-system");
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("owner")).isEqualTo("Digital Channels");
assertThat(ibs.getPropertyNames()) // (1)
    .containsExactlyInAnyOrder("id", "type", "owner", "placeholders");
assertThat(ibs.getLink()).isEqualTo("data:page/id,containers");
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("budget")).isEmpty(); // (2)

  1. getPropertyNames() returns the names of the attributes that hold properties, id and placeholders included. The attributes with getters of their own, label, tooltip, link, tags, style and visible, are left out.

  2. A property that is not set is an empty string, not null. Check with isEmpty() or Util.isBlank.

The label, the tooltip and the link are properties too: getProperty("tooltip") returns the same as getTooltip(). A property name that contains a slash is a property path, which reads a property of a related element (Chapter 9).

8.2. Placeholders

Draw.io replaces %name% in labels and tooltips with the value of the element’s property name, when the element has Placeholders checked in its Edit Data dialog, which sets the property placeholders to 1. The API does the same, in labels and in every property it returns:

Listing 21. Placeholders, and the style of an element as properties
assertThat(ibs.getTooltip()).isEqualTo( // (1)
    "Allows customers to view information about their bank accounts and make payments."
    + " Owned by Digital Channels.");

ibs.setProperty("colors", "Filled with %$style:fillColor%"); // (2)
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("colors")).isEqualTo("Filled with #1168BD");

  1. The tooltip in the file is "…​ Owned by %owner%.", and owner is a property of the same element.

  2. %$style:key% is the value of a key of the element’s style. Here the property colors is set from code, then read back with its placeholder expanded.

A placeholder is resolved in this order:

  1. $style:key, from the element’s style;

  2. if the document was loaded with an EvaluatingContext, the context evaluates the name, which is a hook for an expression language of your choice;

  3. otherwise the element’s own property of that name;

  4. if that is empty, the same property of the element’s parent, its parent’s parent, and so on up to the root;

  5. and finally the document’s context, which can supply properties of its own.

Values are expanded recursively, and a placeholder that refers to itself, directly or through others, is expanded once. A placeholder that resolves to nothing is left as it is, so a missing value shows in the result rather than disappearing. A placeholder names a property, not a property path: %../owner% does not read the parent’s owner (Chapter 9).

8.3. Properties from outside the diagram

The last step of the resolution is the Context the document was loaded with. A context’s getProperty supplies values that are not in the diagram at all: the environment, the release, the region. Context.fromPropertySource makes one from any Function<String, String>, and a map’s get is one:

Listing 22. Properties from the context
Map<String, String> environment = Map.of("environment", "production");
Context context = Context.fromPropertySource(environment::get); // (1)
Document document;
try (InputStream in = Diagrams.class.getResourceAsStream("internet-banking.png")) {
    List<Document> documents = Document.loadFromPngMetadata(in, null, context);
    document = documents.getFirst();
}

ModelElement<?> api = document.getModelElementById("api");
api.setProperty("placeholders", "1");
api.setProperty("endpoint", "https://api.%environment%.example.com"); // (2)
assertThat(api.getProperty("endpoint")).isEqualTo("https://api.production.example.com");

  1. Every load method has a variant that takes a context.

  2. The API Application inherits nothing from its parents, so %environment% comes from the context.

The same diagram loaded with another context reads differently, and nothing in the file changes. That is a way to use one diagram for several environments, or for several releases, with links and labels that follow them.

8.4. Filtering properties

A context also sees every property as it is read, and can change it. filterProperty receives the element, the name and the value, and returns the value to use. Filters suit cross-cutting rules, such as redaction for a diagram published outside the team:

Listing 23. A context that hides owners
Context publicRendering = new Context() {

    @Override
    public String filterProperty(ModelElement<?> element, String name, String value) {
        return "owner".equals(name) ? null : value; // (1)
    }

};
Document document;
try (InputStream in = Diagrams.class.getResourceAsStream("internet-banking.png")) {
    document = Document.loadFromPngMetadata(in, null, publicRendering).getFirst();
}
ModelElement<?> ibs = document.getModelElementById("ibs");
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("owner")).isNull();
assertThat(ibs.getProperty("type")).isEqualTo("software-system");

  1. The filter sees every value read through getProperty, the tooltip and the link included, before its placeholders are expanded. Labels, and the values that placeholders pull in, are read without it.

Contexts compose: Context.from(a, b) and a.compose(b) ask a first and fall back to b, run both filters in turn, and open streams with whichever context can.

8.5. Writing properties

setProperty writes a property, and setting it to null or an empty string removes it. An element that has no properties yet is a bare mxCell in the file; the first property wraps it in an object element, as Draw.io does.

Listing 24. Writing properties
ModelElement<?> mainframe = document.getModelElementById("mainframe");
mainframe.setProperty("retirement", "2028");
mainframe.setTooltip(mainframe.getTooltip() + ". Retirement planned for 2028");
mainframe.setProperty("owner", null); // (1)
assertThat(mainframe.getPropertyNames()).contains("retirement").doesNotContain("owner");
assertThat(document.save(false)).contains("retirement=\"2028\"");

  1. null removes the property.

Writing properties from code is how a program puts what it learned back into the diagram, where people see it: an owner looked up in a directory, a retirement date from a portfolio, a status from a deployment. Chapter 11 does the same with styles.

Three defects found while writing this chapter, each reproduced against 2026.11.0:

  • select("link-target") on an element without a link returns null, not an empty list, and a property path through it, such as getProperty("link-target/owner"), throws a NullPointerException. Chapter 10. Links between pages, elements and documents

Links are how a diagram gets levels of detail: an element on a system context page links to the page that shows what is inside it. Draw.io links an element to a URL or to a page of the same file. The API reads those links, and extends them to elements, and to pages and elements of other documents, so a set of files becomes one network of diagrams.

10.1. Link syntax

Draw.io writes a link to a page as data:page/id,<page ID>. The API accepts four forms, two of which are Draw.io’s own:

Link Points to

data:page/id,<page>

A page by ID. Draw.io writes this form when you choose a page in the Edit Link dialog.

data:page/name,<page>

A page by name, URL-encoded: data:page/name,System+Context.

data:element/id,<element>

An element by ID.

data:element/name,<element>

An element by the text of its label, HTML removed, URL-encoded.

The part after the comma can say where to look:

  • <document>#<page> for a page in another document, where <document> is a URI resolved against the URI of the linking document: data:page/name,overview.drawio#Page+2;

  • [<document>#]<page selector>/<element> for an element on another page, where the page selector is id,<page ID> or name,<page name>: data:element/id,id,system-context/ibs, data:element/name,overview.drawio#name,Page+2/Linked.

An element link without a page selector looks on the linking element’s own page. Element links and links to other documents are an extension: Draw.io itself opens them as URLs, so they work in programs built on the API and not when clicked in the editor or the viewer.

10.2. Resolving links

getLinkTarget() returns what a link points to: a Page or a ModelElement, both LinkTarget`s. `isTargetLink() says whether the link is in one of the four forms at all; a link to a web page returns false, and its target is null.

Listing 30. Links within the running example (LinkTests.java)

  1. The Internet Banking System links to the Containers page, by ID, as Draw.io wrote it.

  2. The boundary on the Containers page links back to the Internet Banking System, by page ID and element ID.

  3. The Mainframe Banking System on the Containers page links to its namesake on the System Context page, by page name and element ID.

A link that cannot be resolved, to a page or an element that does not exist, throws a ConfigurationException with a marker that names the page and the element that has the link. That makes checking a diagram’s links a one-line test: visit every element with isTargetLink() and call getLinkTarget().

The URIs in links do not have to be locations. A context’s openStream(URI) opens every linked document, so it can map logical URIs to wherever the documents are: a classpath, a Maven artifact, a GitLab repository read through its API without cloning, or a different location for each environment:

10.6. Element URIs

getURI() returns a URI for every element: the document’s URI with a fragment of the IDs from the page down, so it identifies the element across documents. resolveURI(base, provider, connectionBase) builds URIs of your own, from a property of each element resolved against its logical parent’s, with the connection base of Chapter 7 deciding whether a connection’s parent is its layer, its source or its target. That is how a diagram maps to a hierarchy of resources, such as the pages of a documentation site, or the paths of an API.

Chapter 11. Creating and changing diagrams

A program that writes diagrams puts what it knows where people look. It can generate a diagram from scratch, from a system, an inventory or a model, or change one that people drew, to mark what a check found or what a deployment did, and leave the rest of the drawing as it was.

A new document has no pages; a new page has a model, a root and one layer. Nodes are created in a layer, or in another node:

11.2. Styles

An element’s style is the style attribute in the file, a list of key=value; pairs, and getStyle() returns it as a live Map<String, String>. Changing the map changes the attribute. Style, LineStyle, NodeStyle and ConnectionStyle add typed, fluent methods for common keys on top of the map:

  • Style: opacity, rounded, shadow, enumerate and enumerateValue;

  • LineStyle: color, the stroke, width and dashed;

  • NodeStyle: backgroundColor, the fill, shape, align, verticalAlign, fontSize, fontColor, fontStyle, labelBackgroundColor, labelBorderColor, rotation, container and collapsible;

  • ConnectionStyle: edgeStyle, startArrow, endArrow, startFill, endFill and jump, with the Arrow and Jump enums for the common values.

The typed methods are a convenience, not a complete list. Draw.io has hundreds of style keys, and any of them can be put into the map directly: style.put("gradientColor", "#7EA6E0"). The easiest way to find the key for an effect is to apply it in Draw.io and look at Edit Style.

Chapter 12. Geometry and order

The elements of a diagram come in the order of the file, which is the order in which they were drawn. That order rarely means anything. The order people see does: the steps of a process clockwise around a hub, the stages of a pipeline from left to right, the numbered steps of a sequence, the flow of the arrows. A program that turns a diagram into a list, a table of contents, a sequence of slides, or the steps of a plan needs that order, and the module’s comparators compute it.

LabelModelElementComparator compares the text of labels, HTML removed, and PropertyModelElementComparator compares a property, both with empty values last. FlowComparator counts the steps along connections from one element to the other, so it orders a chain by its arrows; a predicate restricts the connections it follows. EnumerateComparator compares Draw.io’s enumeration values as paths of numbers and words, so 1.10 comes after 1.9 and before 2.

12.2. Geometry

Listing 43. Orders by angle and by position
assertThat(sort(steps, new AngularNodeComparator(release, null))) // (1)
    .containsExactly("Plan", "Deploy", "Test", "Build");
assertThat(sort(steps, new AngularNodeComparator(release, null).reversed()))
    .containsExactly("Build", "Test", "Deploy", "Plan");
assertThat(sort(steps, new CartesianNodeComparator(Direction.rightDown))) // (2)
    .containsExactly("Deploy", "Plan", "Test", "Build");
assertThat(sort(steps, new CartesianNodeComparator(Direction.downRight)))
    .containsExactly("Plan", "Deploy", "Build", "Test");

  1. By angle around the hub, starting at twelve o’clock and going counterclockwise, as angles do in mathematics.

  2. By columns from left to right, then by rows from top to bottom. Nodes that overlap horizontally are in the same column.

AngularNodeComparator takes the hub and a base angle in radians, which defaults to π/2, twelve o’clock. Reversing it gives a clockwise order, but one that starts just before the base angle, so the step at twelve o’clock comes last; to start a clockwise order at twelve o’clock, reverse it with a base angle a little past twelve o’clock. CartesianNodeComparator has eight directions: rightDown and downRight above, and the other combinations of right or left with down or up. Geometry is absolute: a node inside a container is compared by its position on the page.

12.3. Enumeration

Draw.io’s Enumerate checkbox puts a badge on an element, and the badge shows a number that Draw.io computes, or the value of enumerateValue when the style has one.

Listing 44. Enumeration in the style

12.4. Comparators that are not orders

FlowComparator and EnumerateComparator do not define a total order on every set of elements. Two elements that no path connects are equal to the flow comparator, and so are two elements without enumeration values to the enumeration comparator, which treats them as equal to everything. Java’s sorting assumes a consistent order, and on such sets it gives an order that depends on the input. Chain them with a comparator that is total, thenComparing(new PositionModelElementComparator()) for the order of the file or the label comparator, and keep cycles out of what you sort by flow.

Chapter 13. Rendering in a browser

A diagram that a program built or changed has no picture until something draws it (Chapter 11). In Draw.io that does not matter, because the editor draws a diagram as it opens it. A documentation build, a report or a video needs the picture as a file. This chapter renders pages as PNG and SVG images, clicks shapes before taking the picture, and records videos of pages shown in sequence.

13.1. Why draw.io’s own viewer

A .drawio file does not hold everything that Draw.io shows. Draw.io computes much of it each time it draws:

  • Edge routes, from the style. An orthogonal edge often stores few points or none, and line jumps and loops are computed too.

  • Where an edge meets a shape, from the shape’s outline and the edge’s exit and entry constraints.

  • Labels, which are usually HTML that the browser wraps.

Drawing a diagram in Java would mean rebuilding all of that, and keeping up with Draw.io as it changes. Instead, the module runs Draw.io’s own viewer, the script that shows diagrams embedded in web pages, in a browser that Playwright starts and drives. The picture is what the person who drew the diagram saw, because it is drawn by the same code. The price is a browser.

13.2. The module

Rendering is in a module of its own, org.nasdanika.sdk.runtime:drawio-playwright, so that the drawio module stays light: Playwright and a browser come only with this one. It is in this book because it is about Draw.io documents.

A rendering launches Chromium, in one of two ways:

13.3. A page as PNG

  1. The first page of the document, cropped to the diagram and a border of 10 pixels, written to the file.

  2. The image carries the page, as Draw.io’s own PNG export does with Include a copy of my diagram: loadFromPngMetadata reads it back, and Draw.io opens the image for editing.

The embedded document has only the page in the picture. A person who opens the image in Draw.io edits that page, and saves the image again with the page in it, which is what the figures of this book are.

13.4. A page as SVG

Listing 48. Rendering a page as SVG
Page containers = document.getPageByName("Containers");
Path file = rendering().svg(containers, OUTPUT.resolve("containers.svg"));

try (InputStream in = Files.newInputStream(file)) {
    Document embedded = Document.loadFromSvg(in, null); // (1)

}

  1. The SVG carries the page in the content attribute of its root element, as Draw.io’s editable SVG export does. Document.loadFromSvg reads it.

The SVG is the one Draw.io exports: shapes and edges as SVG elements with data-cell-id attributes naming their cells, and HTML labels as foreignObject elements. Browsers draw foreignObject. Some other renderers do not, among them the one Asciidoctor PDF uses, so a PDF takes the PNG.

13.5. Several pages

Listing 49. Rendering pages by name, at scale 2
List<Page> pages = Pages.byName(document, "Context", "Containers"); // (1)
List<Path> files = rendering()
    .withScale(2) // (2)
    .png(pages, OUTPUT.resolve("pages"), Rendering.fileNames(".png")); // (3)
assertThat(files).extracting(f -> f.getFileName().toString()).containsExactly("context.png", "containers.png");

  1. Pages by name, in the order given. Pages.byId picks them by ID, and Pages.all takes them all. A missing page is an error, not a gap.

  2. Twice as many pixels in each direction, through the browser’s device scale factor. The diagram is drawn at that resolution, not enlarged afterwards.

  3. A file per page, named by a function of the page: Rendering.fileNames(".png") names a page’s file after its name, in lower case with words joined by hyphens.

The pages are rendered in one browser session: the viewer is loaded once and shows each page in turn. svg takes the same arguments.

13.6. Interacting before the picture

A page can be changed in the browser before its picture is taken. The last argument of png and svg is a function that receives the viewer and the page once the viewer shows the page:

Listing 50. Clicking a button that runs custom actions
Path directory = OUTPUT.resolve("payment");
Rendering rendering = rendering().withScale(2);
List<Page> pages = Pages.byName(document, "Payment");
List<Path> files = rendering.png(pages, directory, Rendering.fileNames("-after.png"), (viewer, page) -> {
    viewer.png(directory.resolve("payment-before.png")); // (1)
    viewer.click("pay-button"); // (2)
    viewer.session().pause(Duration.ofMillis(1500)); // (3)
});

  1. A picture of the page as it is first shown. The function can take as many as it likes.

  2. A click in the middle of the shape of the cell with the ID pay-button, as a person would click it. The button’s link holds custom actions, which start flow animations on the two connections and color the ledger.

  3. Time for the actions to run. The pause is in the browser session’s API, with the rest of Playwright.

13.7. Videos

A video shows pages in sequence, each for as long as its frame says:

Listing 51. A tour of three pages
Page context = document.getPageByName("Context");
Page containers = document.getPageByName("Containers");
Page payment = document.getPageByName("Payment");
Path video = rendering().video(OUTPUT.resolve("tour.webm"), List.of(
        Frame.of(context, Duration.ofSeconds(2)), // (1)
        Frame.of(containers, Duration.ofSeconds(2)),
        Frame.of(payment, Duration.ofSeconds(3), (viewer, page) -> viewer.click("pay-button")))); // (2)
assertThat(video).isNotEmptyFile();

  1. A frame: a page and how long it is shown.

  2. A frame with a function, which runs once the page is shown: here it clicks the button, and the actions play while the frame lasts.

The video is WebM, at the size of the browser’s viewport, 1280 by 800 pixels by default. Each page is fit to the viewport and centered, enlarged at most twice. The recording starts when the browser opens its page, so the video begins with the fraction of a second the viewer takes to load.

Draw.io animations are what videos are for: flow animations along connections, and custom actions that show, hide, fade, highlight and restyle shapes. In the viewer they run only when a person clicks, and in a video they run on cue, the same way every time.

13.9. Limits

  • The viewer comes from jsDelivr, at a pinned version, so rendering needs the network. It is downloaded once per JVM and served to the browser from memory. Shipping the viewer as a resource, for builds without the network, is PLANNED.

  • Fonts are the machine’s. Draw.io’s default font is Helvetica, and a browser draws it with whatever it substitutes: Arial on Windows, Liberation Sans on Linux. The same page can come out a few pixels different on two machines, so tests compare embedded documents, not pixels.

  • What the viewer does not draw is not in the picture: shapes from stencil libraries that the viewer does not include, or images that cannot be loaded.

Part III: Looking Ahead

The points of the continuum that this module does not reach yet, and how the runtime will reach them.

Chapter 14. The rest of the continuum

This module covers the middle of the continuum: a diagram as data, read, checked, changed and written by programs, and shared as an interactive page. The points to its right exist in Nasdanika core, built on its graph, mapping and HTML application modules. In the runtime they will be rebuilt on its own foundation, and this chapter says how, as far as it is designed. Everything in it is PLANNED unless it says otherwise.

14.1. Documented diagrams

A documentation site generated from a diagram: a page per documented element, a navigation tree from the diagram’s containment and links, the diagrams embedded in the viewer and linked to the pages, and search. In Nasdanika core, the drawio html-app site command of the Nasdanika CLI, and a GitHub template repository that runs it, generate such sites, from documentation in properties, from Markdown files that elements refer to, and from prototypes that mount other sites.

In the runtime, sites will be generated with its HTML and Bootstrap modules, which its own site is planned to be generated with as well. The diagram supplies the tree, the element properties supply the pages, and the API of this book reads both.

14.2. Executable diagrams

Behavior bound to diagram elements: a property names the code an element stands for, and the code is wired to its neighbors along the diagram’s connections. Nasdanika core does this with graph processors, which exchange handlers and endpoints along connections, synchronously or concurrently, and are created from invocable URIs in element properties.

The design for the runtime is open. Its natural foundation is the runtime’s capability framework, which finds and creates the code that a requirement names, with the diagram as the wiring, and its telemetry, which would record what the diagram did.

14.3. Diagrams as models

Diagram elements mapped to the objects of a typed model, so that a diagram is one of the sources of a model alongside spreadsheets, scripts and code. In Nasdanika core, semantic mapping does this: element properties name the classes and features of an Ecore metamodel, and a diagram loaded into a resource set is a model.

The runtime takes a different road there, and the first part of it is built, in modules beside this one AVAILABLE:

  • The Draw.io model, models/drawio, is an EMF model of Draw.io documents. A .drawio file, or a PNG or SVG image that Draw.io exported with the diagram in it, loads into any resource set of the runtime as a model, through a resource factory that the module contributes, and saves back. It reads and writes with the API of this book. Its Zephyr Book is Draw.io Model.

  • The diagram model, models/diagram, which the Draw.io model extends, gives every element semantic elements: references to the objects of other models that the element depicts. Its Zephyr Book, Diagram Model, maps the figure of its own metamodel, drawn in Draw.io, to that metamodel and checks one against the other.

What remains PLANNED is the mapping itself without code: element properties that name classes and features, as in core, so that a diagram loaded into a resource set is a model of the domain and not only of the diagram. Until then, a mapping is a few lines of Java over the diagram model, or a Groovy script: the runtime’s Groovy DSL can read a Draw.io resource while it builds a model.

14.4. One diagram model, many pictures

Most of this is built AVAILABLE. The runtime’s diagram model holds what notations have in common: nodes, ports, connections, layers, nesting and labels, independent of any editor. A Draw.io document is one: its pages are slides of the presentation model, and slides are diagrams.

  • Layout, with Eclipse ELK, is written once for the diagram model, in models/diagram/elk, and lays out Draw.io pages because they are diagrams. The Draw.io Model book generates a diagram of the modules of a running JVM, lays it out with ELK and renders it with Draw.io’s viewer (Chapter 13).

  • Rendering goes to React Flow, where a diagram should be interactive, to Draw.io’s own viewer, where a diagram should look exactly as drawn, and to SVG, through the runtime’s SVG model, where it should be vector graphics in a document. The Zephyr Books Diagram Layout with ELK and SVG Model show them.

PLANNED: routes in from other notations. The presentation model, for slides, is a sketch, and models of Visio, PowerPoint and Excel files are postponed.

The same path makes diagrams scenes of narrated videos: a diagram on screen, with elements highlighted as a narration mentions them, regenerated when the diagram changes PLANNED.

14.5. Rendering generated diagrams

Rendering is built: Chapter 13 renders pages with Draw.io’s own viewer, as PNG and SVG images with the page embedded and as videos. What remains PLANNED is an Asciidoctor extension that renders a .drawio file referenced from a book as the book is built, so that the source of a figure in Git is the diagram rather than its image, and the viewer shipped as a resource, so that rendering does not need the network.

14.6. Diagrams and AI assistants

A diagram as data is good context for an assistant: a graph of short texts, labels, tooltips and properties, joined by containment, connections and links. An assistant that is given the elements near the one a person asks about, by graph distance as well as by similarity of text, answers questions about an architecture more precisely than one given the whole diagram as an image. The API of this book is enough to build that context today; the runtime’s plans for assistants will build it in.

Document, its Context, the element interfaces, links, selectors, property paths, placeholders with $style, styles, comparators and PNG loading work as they did.

B.2. What was left out

Graph processing. In core, Element, Node and Connection extend the interfaces of Nasdanika’s graph module, so diagrams can be made executable with graph processors and dispatched to @Handler methods. In the runtime, the elements extend nothing outside the module (Chapter 14).

The Ecore model of Draw.io documents. Core’s drawio.model module and Document.toModelDocument() are not in this module. The runtime has a Draw.io model of its own, in models/drawio, built on its diagram and presentation models, with tags as objects and connections navigable from both ends. A document becomes a model by loading the .drawio file, or a PNG or SVG image with the document in it, into a resource set, not by converting a Document (Chapter 14). Its classes are named after the diagram model’s, Document, Page, Layer, Node and Connection, not DrawioDocument and the like as in core.

Semantic mapping. Core’s mapping module, which maps diagram elements to the objects of Ecore models by their properties, is not in the runtime. The runtime’s diagram model has the place for the result, the semantic elements of an element, and mapping by properties is planned (Chapter 14).

Spring Expression Language. The $spel: magic property, data:spel, links and the SpEL evaluation context are gone, together with the dependency on Spring. Document.EvaluatingContext remains as the hook for an expression language: implement evaluate to resolve placeholders with the language of your choice.

Layout. Util.layout and Util.forceLayout, which laid out nodes with JGraphT, are gone with their dependency. In the runtime, layout belongs to the diagram model, with Eclipse ELK, in models/diagram/elk, which lays out the pages of the Draw.io model (Chapter 14).

The capability factories that registered Draw.io as a resource type and as processor factories with core’s capability loader. The Draw.io model registers the resource type now, with every resource set of the runtime.

B.3. Dependencies

Core’s module required java.desktop, the graph, mapping, capability and Draw.io model modules, Ecore, and Apache Commons Lang, most of them transitively. The runtime’s requires common and java.xml transitively, and java.desktop, jsoup, JSON, Commons Codec and Commons Text without exporting them (Chapter 5). Code that used java.desktop or one of these libraries through the Draw.io module has to require it itself.

A line between two connectables. Either end may be missing.

Which element a connection is a logical child of: its parent in the file, its source or its target.

The effort readers spend to get what they need from a diagram.

What a document is loaded with: the streams of linked documents, properties for placeholders, and a filter for properties.

A diagram whose elements carry properties with agreed meanings that programs read.

A PNG or SVG image that carries the diagram it shows, so that Draw.io opens it for editing and the API loads it.

A link from a diagram element to another element, on the same page, another page or another document, in the form data:element/…​.

Entity

A thing a diagram element stands for.

A page of a rendered video, shown for a time, with an optional function that runs once the page is shown.

Layer

A part of a page that is shown and hidden as a whole. Every page has at least one.

A shape. A node can contain nodes and connections.

A %name% in a label or a property, replaced with the value of the property name when the element has placeholders on.

The effort of drawing a diagram and keeping it current.

A path of segments, such as .., child and outgoing-connection, which selects elements relative to an element.

Tag

A name that elements of a page share, so that they can be shown and hidden together.

Viewer

Draw.io’s script that shows diagrams in web pages, with pages, layers, links and custom actions. The module drawio-playwright renders diagrams with it in a browser.

Visual communication continuum

The range of structure a diagram can carry, from a picture to a diagram mapped to a model, with each step adding a little to the same file.
